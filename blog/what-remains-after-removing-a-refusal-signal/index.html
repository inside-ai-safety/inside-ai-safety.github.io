What Remains After Removing a Refusal Signal?

Early experiments on separating refusal from harmfulness—and what a failed generalization test taught us.

By Enyi Jiang·Reviewed by Fan Wu·November 13, 2026

In this article

  • Making refusal and harmfulness come apart
  • Removing a direction and checking what remains
  • How closely do refusal and harmfulness directions align?
  • Testing what generalizes
  • Where we go next
  • Sources

A model refuses a request. What does that tell us?

It tells us something about its response. It does not, by itself, tell us which features led to that response. The model might distinguish harmful intent from harmless intent. It might react to alarming words. Both could produce the same refusal.

In this research project, we investigate a related question: if we remove a refusal-related direction from a model’s internal representations, can we still distinguish harmful requests from safe ones?

So far, experiments across three small language models suggest that information predictive of harmfulness labels can survive this removal. But a harder test exposes an important limitation: the surviving signal may still depend on wording or dataset features.

This is a progress report about both findings. [1]

Making refusal and harmfulness come apart

The first difficulty is experimental design.

If harmful prompts are always refused and safe prompts are always answered, a probe that predicts harmfulness could simply be predicting refusal. The two labels move together.

To introduce a useful contrast, we fine-tuned models to refuse both harmful requests and harmless requests that sound alarming, while answering ordinary harmless requests. This deliberately amplifies over-refusal. It is an experimental setup, not a proposed safety policy.

We then estimated a refusal-related direction by comparing two groups of safe prompts: those assigned refusal responses and those assigned answers. Holding harmfulness constant makes this a more informative comparison than contrasting harmful refusals with harmless answers.

It does not remove every confound. The two safe groups also differ in wording and dataset source. The estimated direction may therefore capture a mixture of refusal-related features and alarming language.

Removing a direction and checking what remains

We collected hidden states at the last prompt position and removed their component along the estimated direction. We then trained a new linear probe to predict harmfulness labels from the remaining representations.

The harmfulness evaluation used a separate, topic-matched dataset, with entire topic groups held out for testing. We also compared the result with deleting a random direction.

The stored results show:

Model and evaluated layer Harmfulness AUROC before deletion After deletion and probe retraining
Qwen2.5-1.5B, layer 13 0.932 0.905
Llama-3.2-1B, layer 7 0.963 0.971
OLMo-2-1B, layer 7 0.949 0.945

AUROC measures how well a probe ranks harmful examples above safe ones; 0.5 corresponds to chance ranking. These scores are not percentages of requests handled safely.

The broad pattern is that removing this direction leaves much of the probe’s predictive performance intact. The small increase for Llama should not be interpreted as a safety improvement. These are individual experimental results, not evidence that deletion improves the model. [2]

Refusal-label probes on the edited safe-prompt representations generally weakened substantially, with repeated data splits helping assess variability. This supports a limited conclusion: the tested harmfulness-label information is not confined to the single direction we removed. It does not prove that all refusal information has been erased.

How closely do refusal and harmfulness directions align?

We also compared the refusal-related direction with a harmfulness-related direction: the difference between average hidden states for harmful and safe prompts in the matched training data. This direction summarizes a contrast between groups; it is not the learned probe’s weight vector.

The angle between the two directions changes across layers. An angle of 0° means they point in the same direction; 90° means they are perpendicular. Qwen and OLMo start closer to perpendicular and become more aligned in deeper layers. Llama shows substantial alignment earlier.

Model Early-layer angle range Late-layer angle range
Qwen2.5-1.5B 80–104° 27–37°
Llama-3.2-1B 51–79° 37–46°
OLMo-2-1B 90–112° 39–44°

Early layers cover the first 30% of model depth; late layers cover the last 40%. Ranges describe variation across layers, not confidence intervals. Source: [5].

For Qwen, the angle is approximately 61° at layer 13 and reaches a minimum of 27.5° at layer 19. The two estimated directions therefore overlap substantially in deeper layers without becoming identical. This is consistent with the deletion results: even when the directions are closely aligned, removing the refusal direction can leave information that a new harmfulness probe can use.

The geometry needs a careful interpretation. Near-perpendicular directions in early layers are not strong evidence of independent concepts, especially where harmfulness is weakly decodable. The harmful-versus-safe contrast can itself include refusal-related features. And after deletion, a remaining mean-difference direction becomes perpendicular to the removed direction by construction—not because the model has learned to separate safety from refusal. The angle describes these measured contrasts; it does not identify a pure harmfulness representation.

Testing what generalizes

A strong score on held-out topics is encouraging. But what happens when the distinction between harmful and safe prompts changes?

For Qwen, we applied the matched-data probe to new dataset pairings. At layer 13, it achieved an AUROC of 0.993 when distinguishing harmful questions from ordinary, neutral-sounding safe instructions.

When the safe examples also sounded alarming, performance was much weaker. On Forbidden Questions versus OR-Bench-Hard, AUROC was 0.508—approximately chance. [3]

These results concern the matched probe’s generalization before deletion. They do not directly measure generalization of the retrained, post-deletion probe. Nevertheless, they challenge the interpretation of the original measurement: if the probe already struggles to distinguish harmful intent from alarming wording, its surviving performance cannot establish a robust safety signal.

The result is consistent with reliance on surface cues or dataset style. It does not establish exactly which features caused the failure.

To test whether a broader training contrast could help, the next experiment included several reasons why safe requests can sound dangerous: ambiguous words, benign intent on a potentially harmful topic, and alarming framing.

We trained on two categories and tested on the third. At Qwen layer 13, AUROC was approximately 0.765, 0.784, and 0.587 across the three held-out categories. [4]

This is partial transfer. The probe learned distinctions that extended beyond some training categories, but performance remained weakest on alarming framing. Reporting only the average would hide that weakness.

These generalization experiments have so far been run on Qwen. The three-model deletion result should not be read as three-model validation of a generalizable harmfulness detector.

Where we go next

A cleaner follow-up would study the original models’ own over-refusals: among similarly alarming but safe prompts, compare those the model refuses with those it answers. This could reduce the confounding introduced by deliberately teaching a cue-based policy.

We also want to test the surviving information under harder distribution shifts and use interventions during generation to investigate whether it affects behavior.

For Inside AI Safety, the value of this project is already methodological. Looking inside a model gives us another source of evidence—but that evidence needs its own tests. A convincing probe score is a starting point for investigation, not a certificate of understanding.
