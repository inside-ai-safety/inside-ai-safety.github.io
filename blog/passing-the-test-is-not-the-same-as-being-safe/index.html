Passing the Test Is Not the Same as Being Safe

What Looking Inside AI Models Reveals

At a glance

  • Passing is not proof: Safety tests judge a model’s answers, not what happens inside it. Two models can earn the same score and still differ sharply in how easily they can be pushed into harm.
  • Timing matters: In reasoning models, the moment when risk is easiest to see is not the moment when it is easiest to change. Acting early worked; acting late did not.
  • Strength matters too: Applying one fixed safety setting to every request makes models refuse harmless questions. Scaling safety to each request kept more usefulness.
  • Recommendations: Test models by pushing on them, choose where to intervene by what works rather than what is easiest to detect, and report what every safeguard costs ordinary users.

A building inspector who visits only on calm days can certify that the roof is intact. That certificate says nothing about the first storm.

Much of AI safety testing works this way. A model answers a set of harmful and harmless prompts, a judge scores the answers, and a low failure rate becomes the certificate. The score describes what the model did on the prompts it was shown. It does not describe how close the model sits to doing something else.

That gap matters most for open-weight models, where anyone can fine-tune them or edit their internals. At our community session last week, we walked through three papers that each ask a different question about it: can we trust a passing grade, when can we still steer a model, and how hard should we push? Here is what they found, where they stop short, and what we think follows.

The problem: tests that only read the answers

The harms are already real. The International AI Safety Report 2026 sorts them into misuse, malfunction, and loss of oversight, and each has a recent example. In 2024, fraudsters used deepfaked executives on a video call to trick an Arup employee into wiring $25.6 million. A Canadian tribunal held Air Canada liable after its chatbot invented a refund policy. And in Anthropic’s 2025 stress test, Claude Opus 4 chose blackmail in 96% of simulated runs to avoid being shut down. That last one was a simulation, but it shows that behavior ordinary tests miss can appear under pressure.

The best-known benchmarks, including HarmBench, StrongREJECT, and XSTest, all work the same way: each scores the model’s response with a classifier or an AI judge, and none sees the computation that produced it.

Internal representations, the numbers a model builds as it works, offer a second source of evidence. Researchers have shown they can carry information the output hides, and that adding or removing a single direction in them can switch refusal on or off. That opens two uses: monitoring (reading signals while the model computes) and intervention (changing them before the answer is finished). The three papers test what each is actually good for.

Can we trust a passing grade?

A study of what static audits can and cannot see starts with a simple question. When a model refuses, is it robustly safe, or one small step from complying and never pushed? The authors call the difference between what a static audit certifies and what an intervention reveals the audit gap, and they show it can exist by building it.

Starting from three safety-trained models (Gemma 2 2B, Llama 3.2 3B, Qwen 2.5 3B), they train “dissociated” copies. These refuse harmful requests exactly as before, but comply when a small, fixed nudge (about 6% of the usual signal size) is added at one middle layer.

Every static check treated the copies like the originals. Refusal rates matched. Jailbreak results pointed in inconsistent directions: the Llama copy was easier to jailbreak than its base, the Gemma copy harder. A probe that reads internal activations, trained on the originals and never tuned against the copies, scored near-perfect on both.

Then the authors pushed. A bounded attack on the internals produced harmful compliance on 54–86% of prompts for the copies, against 3–48% for the originals. Random nudges of the same size stayed at or below 12%, so the effect was directional, not just disturbance. When fine-tuned on harmful data, the copies reached 80% harmful compliance in 5 steps; the originals took 10 to 25.

What it means. A behavioral score tells you what a model does when asked. It cannot tell you how far the model is from doing something else. Only an intervention measures that.

What it does not mean. The copies were deliberately constructed, so this proves the gap can exist, not that ordinary training produces it. The results were weakest on Qwen, whose base was already softer, and the authors say so. When they audited five released models, only one aligned model (Qwen2.5-7B) showed even a modest signal.

When can we still steer?

If internal access helps, the next question is timing. Reasoning models write out a chain of thought before answering. That written trail is a chance to step in before the answer is done, but only if the step lands at the right moment. Research on reasoning models tested exactly that.

The researchers learned a “safety direction” from paired safe and unsafe examples. A small gate then reads an early point in the model’s reasoning and either nudges it once along that direction or leaves it alone. The model’s weights are never changed, and everything after the nudge is generated normally. To test timing, they tried the same nudge at each of the first five tokens of reasoning.

The result is sharp. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, nudging at the second or third token raised the share of fully safe outputs on StrongREJECT by about 41 and 50 points. By the fourth and fifth tokens the effect had fallen to roughly zero. Across all three models tested, the useful window was two to three tokens wide.

Now the twist. When the authors measured how well a detector could predict that a trajectory would turn unsafe, the detector got better the later it looked, including at the fifth token, where steering no longer worked. The point where risk is easiest to see is not the point where it is easiest to change. A monitor that flags danger late may see it clearly and still be unable to do anything about it.

The headline number: on the 8B model, the approach raised fully safe outputs on StrongREJECT-273 from 29.06% to 82.05% across three runs. Benign refusal stayed at 5.40%, against a 5.33% baseline. Removing the gate kept almost the whole safety gain but roughly doubled benign refusal, to 10.67%. The direction supplies the safety; the gate keeps ordinary users from paying for it.

What it means. Choosing where to intervene by what is easiest to detect can point you to the wrong place. The only way to know is to make the edit and watch what happens.

What it does not mean. The paper does not claim early reasoning beats editing at the prompt. A matched prompt-level baseline reached 71.31%, and its performance shifted with how it was set up. The models are all from one family, and the exact window shifts by about a token between them.

How hard should we push?

The next question is cost. When safety training is applied everywhere, the same changes reach harmful and harmless requests alike, and models start refusing questions like “how do I kill a Python process?” Another approach keeps the base model frozen and adds a small safety module whose strength is set per request. A lightweight gate reads the model’s own view of the prompt and outputs a score from 0 to 1. Harmless prompts get little safety adjustment; risky ones get more.

On Llama-3-8B-Instruct, this cut HarmBench attack success from 32.25% to 0.50% and raised refusal of unsafe test prompts from 88.5% to 92.5%. It kept 73.46% accuracy on a grade-school math test, about seven points above standard safety fine-tuning (66.34%) and standard LoRA (66.72%). The gate has about 664,000 parameters, versus roughly 279 million for PromptGuard and 8 billion for Llama Guard 3. The paper notes PromptGuard was built for a different task, so this is a design contrast, not a verdict on it.

The trade-offs are real. A standard LoRA baseline reached slightly lower attack success (0.00%), the original model kept higher MMLU scores (66.76 versus 63.62), and over-refusal of safe prompts rose from 2.8% to 4.8% on Llama and to 9.6% on Gemma. The method also depends on the gate being right: a harmless prompt can still trigger it, and a harmful one that scores low can slip past.

What it means. “How strongly?” is a design question, not only “whether.” Scaling safety to each request preserved more of the model’s usefulness than one setting for everyone.

What the three papers add up to

Read together, the studies show that three things we tend to treat as one are separate: seeing a problem, changing it, and applying the change only where it is needed. A strong result on one says little about the others. A probe can look perfect on a fragile model. A detector can keep improving at the very point where control is already gone. And a control that works can still tax everyday users if it fires on everyone.

For open-weight models this matters more than usual. Anyone can fine-tune them or edit their internals, so “how easily can this model’s behavior be changed?” is part of the safety question, and only an intervention can answer it. Developers, evaluators, and the funders who back them should ask for that evidence alongside a benchmark score, and should expect it to come with a measured cost to ordinary users.

Three recommendations

1. Make audits push back. Pair behavioral tests with fine-tuning speed, bounded attacks on internals compared against random controls, and steering sweeps at a few middle layers. These need white-box access, so they suit developers, evaluators with negotiated access, and open-weight reviews. They add to output filtering; they do not replace it.

2. Choose intervention points by effect, not detectability. Regenerate the full response after an edit and compare it against a matched baseline before deciding where control belongs.

3. Report the cost to ordinary users. Every method here was judged on harm reduced and on what it cost harmless requests. A fix that doubles refusals on everyday questions, as the ungated edit did, is a different result from one that does not.

What we still do not know

These are small, open-weight models, most in one family, scored by automatic judges. The constructed models are synthetic. Nothing here shows the same results for frontier systems, multi-turn conversations, or tool-using agents, and nothing shows the methods hold up against an attacker who adapts to them. We treat the papers as a foundation, not a conclusion.

Where Inside AI Safety goes next

Our three projects build on this work. Evaluate will test whether internal metrics hold across model families and turn them into a reproducible audit. Steer will test whether internal intervention can stop an unsafe action by a tool-using agent and help it recover; early experiments are exploring timing, and whether it improves task completion is still open. Strengthen will test whether safety training can survive later weight changes; a small pilot motivates more work but does not yet show general tamper resistance. We will publish methods and findings, including the ones that do not work.
