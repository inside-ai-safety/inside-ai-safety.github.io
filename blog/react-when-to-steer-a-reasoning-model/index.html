Seeing the Risk Is Not the Same as Stopping It

What REACT teaches us about when—and whether—to intervene in a reasoning model.

By Enyi Jiang·Reviewed by Hanwen Zhang·November 19, 2026

In this article

  • A chance to intervene before the answer
  • How REACT works
  • Timing changes the result
  • How does REACT compare with other approaches?
  • Why not steer every request?
  • What this means for AI safety

Imagine watching an AI model work toward an unsafe answer. As it continues, the warning signs become clearer. You might expect that clearer warning signs also make it easier to step in and change the outcome.

Our research suggests that this connection is not so simple.

In REACT: Representational Early Analysis and Control of Chain-of-Thought Trajectories, we study a specific question: when can a single change to a model’s internal state steer its reasoning toward a safer result?

We found that later states can make risk easier to detect, even after the tested intervention has become much less effective. Seeing where a model is heading and being able to redirect it are different abilities.

A chance to intervene before the answer

Some reasoning models generate an intermediate reasoning trace before producing their final answer. This is often called a chain of thought, or CoT.

The model generates this text one token at a time. A token is a small unit of text, such as a word or part of a word. At each step, the model also computes internal numerical representations, often called hidden states.

REACT works with these hidden states. It makes one targeted internal adjustment early in generation, then lets the model continue. The base model’s weights stay unchanged.

The question is whether that single adjustment can change what comes next.

How REACT works

REACT has two main components: a learned direction and a gate.

The direction specifies how to adjust the hidden state. We learn it using paired examples of safe and unsafe reasoning for the same request, refining the adjustment to favor safe continuations over unsafe ones.

The gate decides whether to apply that adjustment. It is trained separately to distinguish requests that should receive an intervention from those that should be left alone.

During use, the process is straightforward:

  1. Let the model begin generating its reasoning.
  2. Read an early hidden state.
  3. Use the gate to choose between one adjustment and no adjustment.
  4. Let the model generate the remaining reasoning and answer normally.

Timing changes the result

To test timing, we applied interventions at different positions among the first five reasoning tokens. We learned a direction for each position and regenerated everything after the edit, so the experiment measured changes to the model’s actual continuation.

On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, the second and third reasoning tokens were particularly effective intervention points. By the fourth and fifth tokens, the safety benefit was approximately zero under the tested method.

A separate detector showed a different pattern: on the 8B model, the fifth-token hidden state was more informative about risk than the third-token state.

The model’s direction became easier to read at a point where our steering method was no longer effective.

This does not mean every model has a universal “third-token safety switch,” or that later intervention is impossible. The experiments used a fixed intervention scale within each model, and the actual size of the edits could differ across positions. The result identifies an early window for this particular method and setup.

How does REACT compare with other approaches?

We compared REACT with the original model and several ways of steering its internal representations. CAST conditionally applies a steering direction, while AlphaSteer learns an input-dependent refusal direction. Prompt-boundary steering applies a gated adjustment before the model begins generating its reasoning. We also tested REACT’s adjustment without a gate, applying it to every request.

For the main comparison, we used the same 8B model and evaluation sets across three generation seeds. CAST and AlphaSteer were adapted to the same supervision resources, making this a controlled comparison within our setup.

Higher safe-output rates and usefulness are better; lower refusal of harmless requests is better. “Safe” requires both the reasoning and final answer to pass the automated safety evaluation. Harmless-request results use Benign-500. Values are means over three generation seeds, rounded to one decimal place; Table 1 of the paper reports the variation across runs. On StrongREJECT, REACT and steering-only produced identical outputs in this evaluation, so their equal scores are not independent evidence.

REACT performed best among the gated methods on both harmful-request sets in this comparison. But these numbers do not establish a universal ranking: the CAST and AlphaSteer runs are matched-data adaptations, not reproductions of every setting in their original papers.

Prompt-boundary steering was also strong. REACT’s results therefore support testing early reasoning states as useful intervention points—not assuming that intervening during reasoning always beats intervening beforehand.

Why not steer every request?

An intervention that makes a model refuse everything could look safe while becoming much less useful.

Removing REACT’s gate helps reveal this trade-off. Steering every request achieved the same 82.1% safety score on StrongREJECT in this experiment. But it increased refusal of harmless requests from about 5.3% to 10.7%.

With the gate, benign refusal stayed at 5.4%, close to the original model. Measured usefulness on the harmless instructions also remained near baseline.

In this setting, the adjustment supplied the safety benefit, while the gate helped avoid unnecessary changes to harmless requests.

What this means for AI safety

A good risk detector is useful, but it does not automatically tell us where an intervention will work best. We need to test both: can we recognize the risk, and can our response still change the outcome?

REACT provides evidence for one way to connect internal monitoring with intervention. Its results come from three models in the DeepSeek-R1 distilled family, using automated evaluations. They do not establish the same behavior in frontier models, long conversations, or agents taking actions with tools. Preserving usefulness on ordinary instructions also does not establish that every capability is preserved.

For Inside AI Safety, a natural next question is whether internal signals can help us intervene before a tool-using agent takes an unauthorized action, while still allowing it to complete legitimate tasks. That is a direction for future research.

The lesson from REACT is simple: when evaluating a safeguard, ask not only how clearly it sees a problem, but what it can still do about it.
